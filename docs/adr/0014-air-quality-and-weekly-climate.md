# The Comfort Index counts air, and climate normals reach every city and every week

The Comfort Index was temperature comfort minus a rain penalty, so it could not see Chiang Mai's February to April burning season or Delhi's November smog. It is now temperature comfort minus a rain penalty minus an air penalty: monthly mean PM2.5 at or below 10 µg/m³ costs nothing, rising linearly to 40 points at 75 µg/m³ and above (75 is the WHO 24-hour interim target 1, and 10 sits under the US EPA "moderate" edge). The terms live in one pure module, `src/lib/comfort.ts`, shared by server and client, and every place that explains the index shows the same three rows: the city panel, the When to go popover and the Repeat a trip popover (`ComfortBreakdown`). A month with no air figure takes nothing off and says "no air-quality data yet" rather than reading as clean air; the stored scores of the 120 seed cities are unchanged until the backfill reaches them. PM2.5 comes from the Open-Meteo Air Quality API (Copernicus CAMS global, about 40 km grid), averaged over 2023 to 2025: the API serves the global model only from mid-2022, so the air window (3 years) is shorter than the weather window (2015 to 2024) and both are labelled. The coarse grid smooths local peaks, so a burning-season city reads lower than a street-level monitor would; the penalty is a floor, not a measurement. Validated in `airQuality.validation.test.ts` against the committed real values: Chiang Mai's March PM2.5 is more than double its August figure and costs it points, Delhi and Lahore are in the unhealthy band in November and peak around the turn of the year, and Honolulu is clean in every month with no penalty. A heat index was not added: the fetch carries no humidity, and adding the variable would raise every call's quota weight by a third.

Week-of-year normals (52 weeks) come from the same daily data: the mean of every day in a week bucket across the ten years, rain as a 7-day total, then a circular [1,2,1]/4 smoothing. A fixed 365-day calendar counts 29 February as 28 February so weeks line up across leap years, and the last eight days of December fold into week 52. A weekly profile exists only when every week has enough days; a sparse series yields none, never a partial one. Weekly PM2.5 is built the same way from daily means of the hourly series. Weeks are scored with the monthly formula after scaling rain by 365.25/12/7. The smoothing is a statistic of real daily data, not interpolation of monthly means, which ADR 0005 rightly rejected.

Storage is one record per 0.25 degree climate cell (`data/climate-normals.json`, one cell per line, integers ×10 for temperature and PM2.5), so cities in the same cell share one record and one fetch (5,878 cities fall in 5,463 cells). A cell holds monthly temperature, rain and PM2.5 and the three weekly arrays; readers derive scores from it with the shared formula. The old `data/seasonality.json` seed and the on-demand `seasonality-runtime.json` stay readable and are superseded city by city as cells arrive, so no reader had to migrate: `MonthSeasonality` only gained an optional `pm25`. Cells fetched by the server (the nightly job, and the weekly profile of any on-demand fetch) go through the document store (`cache/climate-runtime.json`), so both backends work.

Coverage is bounded by Open-Meteo's free tier, 600 weighted calls per minute, 5,000 per hour and 10,000 per day, where a call weighs (variables ÷ 10) × (days ÷ 14). Ten years of three daily variables weighs about 78 and three years of hourly PM2.5 about 8, so one cell costs about 86 and the free tier allows roughly 80 cells a day inside our own ceilings (400 a minute, 3,500 an hour, 7,000 a day, configurable); covering all 5,463 cells takes about 70 days. The minute limit was confirmed by hitting it at one call every 1.5 seconds, which is why the pacing is in weighted calls, not requests. `npm run backfill:climate` and the server's nightly job (`CLIMATE_JOB_*`, default 03:00 UTC, 2,500 weighted calls a run) are the same code: plan the gaps (cells with nothing yet, seed cities first, then capitals and large-airport cities, then by population; then cells missing only air; then the oldest stale ones, after two years), spend within a persisted ledger of the last 24 hours of calls, save each cell the moment it lands, and stop when the provider answers with a limit. A failed or limited call writes nothing. Run in the build sandbox for the length of the session, the script's committed result is partial and the number is in the README's known gaps; the job closes the rest unattended. Rejected: `temperature_2m_mean` (a third cheaper, but it changes the 120 existing scores' method); a commercial key (the app has no budget line for it); coarser 0.5 degree cells (barely fewer calls, visibly merges coastal and highland neighbours); and fabricating or interpolating cells from neighbours.

Repeat a trip gains `resolution: "week"`. A city with weekly normals contributes week cells, scored on comfort, temperature and rain (monthly-equivalent) plus the week of the year, circular over 52 weeks and normalised at 26; a city without them is matched by month exactly as before, labelled "by month", so the two kinds rank together honestly. The template's week is its fingerprint's `weekOfYear` (ISO week; 53 folds into 52, and ISO and bucket weeks can differ by one), else the middle of its month, shifted by the month bend at 52/12 weeks per month. Because 52 cells per city would let one city's adjacent weeks fill the 60 stage-1 survivors, a city keeps at most 3 weeks, 4 or more weeks apart. The default resolution, the fingerprint, stored watches and every existing result are unchanged; `week`, `resolution`, `weekGrid` and `weeklyUniverseSize` appear only on week queries. Observed fingerprint comfort still has no air term, because there is no observed PM2.5 to fetch; normals-based comfort does. When to go has a Weeks timeframe (52 columns, with the same monthly fallback shown flat and labelled) and an Air metric, and the map has an Air tint layer and "Air quality" conditions, which exclude places with no PM2.5 rather than counting them clean.
